# XXD Shop 009 · Original brief (English reading translation)

> Reading translation only. The canonical runtime prompt is [zh-CN.md](zh-CN.md).

Design a mature, high-conversion e-commerce main visual with a sense of brand for the current product.

If the user provides a product image, strictly keep the product's real appearance, structural proportions, packaging, colour, material, brand text and identifying features, without arbitrarily redrawing or altering it; mainly redesign light, space, background, base, colour scheme, text–image relationship and information hierarchy. Without a product image, build a believable product visual from the category, use, price character, consumption scene and brand attributes.

First judge what this image's most important task is: building brand feel, highlighting the core selling point, reinforcing price advantage, creating appetite, freshness, a sense of quality or gift value. The whole image allows only one strongest information centre; everything else serves it, avoiding several selling points competing for the first glance.

The product must be clear, real and weighty, the main visual subject. Keep the area around it relatively clean; background textures, illustrations, plants, graphics and text must not cross or break the product's outline. The product rests naturally on a tabletop, tray, wood or stone structure, paper, fabric, vessel or other support fitting its character, with real footing, contact shadow and spatial gravity, avoiding floating and templated podiums.

Light centres on shaping the product's volume and material. Let highlights, mid-tones, shadows and contact shadows build layers naturally so the product separates clearly from the background; the subject area is more fully lit, the environment softer, and the background actively recedes rather than everything being equally bright.

The colour scheme unfolds naturally from the product's own colours and consumer semantics, not a mechanical fixed swatch set. Protect the product's original colours first, then decide the environment colours.
When the product is colourful, lower the background's saturation and complexity; when the product is light, build its outline through value, warm-cool, material or local dark contrast; when the product is dark, lift the environment appropriately so the subject does not sink.
Main background, base, supporting graphics and text belong to one colour world but are not identical, forming layers through value, saturation, warm-cool and material differences.
Keep only a few truly attacking accent colours for price, promotion, core tags or the most critical information; never let several highly saturated colours appear together. Price, button and tag colours must come from the product or brand visual relationship, not arbitrary reds, yellows, oranges and greens.

Text and image are not stacked top-and-bottom or used to fill left and right; they compose together. The large title is both text and a large visual shape, with complete, clear, stable letterforms and enough visual weight, placed naturally according to the product's outline, empty space and the image's flow. The title may echo the product in height, size, density and direction, but must not press on the product's key parts.

Supporting text forms clear information clusters rather than scattering into many tags. Group information of the same nature automatically, for example:
product identity with the main title;
core benefits;
secondary selling points;
price, offers, timing and purchase information;
proof information such as origin, specification, ingredients and process.
Different groups are distinguished by size, weight, spacing, base shapes, lines or position, not by adding more colours.

Distil selling points first; do not list them mechanically. The viewer should first quickly understand "what this is" and "the best reason to buy", and only then see price, secondary benefits and proof. Keep secondary selling points concise, similar in kind and uniformly expressed, so each one does not become its own visual centre.

Price presence depends on the product's attributes: heavily promoted, high-frequency products may make price a strong visual anchor; premium, gift and brand-led products let the product and brand character come first, keeping price clear without hard selling. The number itself takes priority over the unit, original price, offer notes and timing, forming a natural information ladder.

The text–image layout may vary freely without a preset template. Text may rest in empty space, wrap the product's outline, echo top and bottom, create left-right tension, partly overlap or use editorial zoning, but must keep a clear reading path, so the viewer grasps the core in a very short time and then naturally moves to the product, price and supporting information instead of searching for an entry point.

The background may be rich, but richness happens outside the subject: natural light and shadow, low-contrast plants, ingredients, textures, environmental cues, abstract graphics, cultural imagery or brand patterns may appear, strengthening the product's meaning rather than adding decoration. The closer to the product and core copy, the cleaner the visual; only further from the core area may more atmospheric information appear.

The final effect should achieve:
clear product logic,
clear background logic,
colour with priority,
text truly integrated with the product,
an independently recognisable price,
concentrated rather than scattered selling points,
rich information, yet the first glance always knows where to look.

Avoid: colours all pushing equally, every selling point highlighted, all text the same size, a screen full of tags, a background stealing from the product, decoration crossing the subject, blurry titles, price and selling points jumbled together, mechanical nine-grid layouts, a fixed-template feel and cheap promotional feel.
